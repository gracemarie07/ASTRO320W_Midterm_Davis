\section*{Introduction}
In this report, I present an analysis of CCD calibration and stellar
imaging observations obtained by the Artemis group with the Davey
Laboratory 24-inch telescope, following the course manual \cite{manual}.
The observations include bias, dark, and flat-field frames, together with
stellar focus sequences, repeated photometric exposures, BVR imaging,
and double-star fields. I use these data to characterize the detector,
examine the stability of the photometric measurements, and determine
the camera's plate scale. The observations were collected with the group;
I carried out the analysis and wrote the report.

\section{Exercise A: CCD Characteristics}
Digital number (DN) and analog-to-digital unit (ADU) refer to the same signal unit. Standard deviation (SD) describes measurement scatter; standard error of the mean (SEM) describes the estimated uncertainty in a mean. Unless noted otherwise,
regional measurements use a central $100\times100$-pixel square
starting at zero-based array coordinates $(1478,1478)$ in the
unbinned $3056\times3056$ images.

\subsection{Bias Frames and Read Noise}
We collected 20 bias frames (0.1-s dark exposures) at the coldest
cooler setting. Two instructor-provided Python routines calculate
\begin{equation}
\sigma_t(x,y)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
[B_i(x,y)-\overline B(x,y)]^2},
\end{equation}
then average over the detector or a selected region. This measures
temporal variation rather than fixed pixel-to-pixel bias structure.
The full-detector result was 9.645 DN with $N=20$ in the denominator
(9.896 DN with $N-1$); the central result was 9.655 DN.

To examine the contribution from fixed bias structure, I formed a
32-bit average master bias in AstroImageJ and subtracted it from each
frame. The mean spatial SD decreased from 13.847 to 9.790 DN
(Table~\ref{tab:a_bias_statistics}), indicating that a substantial
fraction of the single-frame scatter is associated with a stable spatial
pattern. Because each frame also contributes to the master, the residual
variance is reduced:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(B_i-\overline B)=\sigma_{\rm RN}^2(1-1/N).
\end{equation}
Correcting for this gives $9.790/\sqrt{19/20}=10.04$ DN.

To investigate whether these properties vary across the detector, I
divided the images into an $8\times8$ grid and repeated the paired
measurement within each block
(Figure~\ref{fig:bias_map}). Block read noise ranges from 9.63 to
10.48 DN. In contrast, the mean single-frame spatial SD ranges from
9.67 to 61.85 DN, with a strong localized excess. Stable bias structure and pixels with unusually large offsets contribute
to the spatial scatter, whereas their fixed contribution cancels in the
pair differences. The contrast between the two maps therefore indicates
that single-frame bias scatter cannot be attributed to read noise alone.

The measured Exercise B gain applies to $2\times2$ binning. For
these $1\times1$ frames I use the header gain,
$g_{\rm hdr}=1.34~\mathrm{e^-\,DN^{-1}}$. The temporal, shared-master,
and paired results correspond to 12.9, 13.1, and $\boxed{13.5~\mathrm{e^-\,pixel^{-1}\,read^{-1}}}$, respectively.
The paired SEM alone contributes 0.009 electron; the header gain's
unknown uncertainty limits the electron conversion.

\subsection{Dark Signal and Exposure Time}
I measured six exposures from 0.1 to 60 s at a reported $0^\circ$C
setpoint. Their actual CCD temperatures were not recorded. The
headers contain neither \texttt{CALSTAT} nor \texttt{PEDESTAL}, so
I treated them as uncorrected. Using the central master-bias mean
$B=1038.930$ DN, I calculated
\begin{equation}
Q=g_{\rm hdr}(D_{\rm raw}-B),\qquad I=Q/t.
\end{equation}
The master bias was obtained at a $-30^\circ$C setpoint, so a
temperature-dependent offset may remain after subtraction. The telescope
was also uncovered, allowing possible stray-light contamination.
Consequently, the measured signal may contain contributions in addition
to intrinsic dark current.

For a formal uncertainty in the regional mean, I used independent
pixel shot noise, read noise, and the finite 20-frame master:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_Q=\frac{g_{\rm hdr}}{\sqrt n}
\sqrt{\frac{\max(D_{\rm net},0)}{g_{\rm hdr}}+
R^2(1+1/N)}.
\end{equation}
Here $n=10000$, $N=20$, $R=10.045$ DN, and
$\sigma_I=\sigma_Q/t$. This model excludes hot-pixel variability,
spatial correlations, bias-temperature mismatch, gain uncertainty,
and stray light. The shared master also correlates different frames.
Table~\ref{tab:a_dark_time} lists the formal errors; the systematic uncertainties remain larger.

The 0.1-s frame gives an apparent rate of
$423~\mathrm{e^-\,pixel^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. A small offset divided by a
short exposure can produce this result, so I show it but exclude
it from the fit. For 6--60 s, the mean rate is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\overline I=13.76\pm0.51~\mathrm{e^-\,pixel^{-1}\,s^{-1}}}.
\end{equation}
The uncertainty is the SEM; the sample SD is 1.14.
An unweighted fit $Q=mt+b$ gives
\begin{align}
m&=14.33\pm0.78~\mathrm{e^-\,pixel^{-1}\,s^{-1}},\\
b&=-14.8\pm28.4~\mathrm{e^-\,pixel^{-1}}.
\end{align}
The quoted fit errors are estimated from the residual scatter. The
approximately linear increase in charge is consistent with a nearly
constant accumulation rate over 6--60 s. However, stray light adds
signal, while the unmatched bias can shift the result in either
direction. The fitted rate therefore describes the observed signal
accumulation rather than a strict upper limit on intrinsic dark current.

\subsection{Temperature Dependence}
Four frames have verified 300-s exposures and recorded starting
temperatures: $-7.84$, 0.84, 18.75, and $20.22^\circ$C
(Table~\ref{tab:a_dark_temperature}). The files labeled
\texttt{-20C.fit} and \texttt{-25C.fit} instead contain 0.12-s and
10-s exposures without recorded CCD temperatures. I excluded them and used the same region, bias, and gain for the four retained frames.

The measured rate increases from 0.042 to
$3.946~\mathrm{e^-\,pixel^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ across the retained
temperature range. However, the two warmest frames differ by almost a
factor of three in signal despite starting only $1.47^\circ$C apart.
This suggests that the starting temperature may not represent the
conditions throughout each exposure. The coldest measurement is also
particularly sensitive to the bias subtraction. To examine whether the
overall trend is consistent with thermal generation, I fitted
\begin{align}
I(T)&=I_0\exp[-A/(kT)],\\
\ln(I/I_{\rm ref})&=c-(A/k)(1/T),
\end{align}
where $T$ is in kelvin,
$k=8.617333\times10^{-5}~\mathrm{eV\,K^{-1}}$, and
$I_{\rm ref}=1~\mathrm{e^-\,pixel^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$.
The unweighted slope is approximately $-1.107\times10^4$ K, giving
$\boxed{A_{\rm app}=0.95\pm0.14~\mathrm{eV}}$. The error follows from the regression
slope error, $\sigma_A=k\sigma_{\rm slope}$. The increase is consistent with thermal generation, but the small sample, unmatched bias, and uncertain temperatures limit the activation-energy estimate.

\section{Exercise B: Flat Fields and Gain}
\subsection{Photon Transfer Curves}
I measured regional means and spatial SDs in AstroImageJ. The sky
sequence contains twenty 6-s R-band frames with regional means of
16048--38285 DN. All measured regions were digitally unclipped;
one frame has three 65535-DN pixels elsewhere, leaving nineteen
entire images free of digital clipping. The dome sequence contains
13 frames at seven exposures (6, 12, 15, 18, 30, 45, and 90 s),
with changing lamp settings. Its headers record acquisition-time
dark subtraction and \texttt{PEDESTAL=-100}. The original PTC
region and pedestal handling were not saved.

For photon-dominated signal $S$, the variance follows
$\sigma^2\simeq S/g$, giving an expected log--log noise-versus-signal
slope of $1/2$. The sky measurements rise steadily with signal,
whereas the dome measurements turn over at high signal
(Figure~\ref{fig:sky_ptc}). Because the measured spatial SD also
contains illumination gradients and pixel-sensitivity structure, these
curves provide a qualitative comparison rather than an independent
precision gain measurement. The dome points were digitized from the
preserved plot and are not used in a numerical fit.

\subsection{Gain from Paired Flats}
We took twenty 0.2-s and twenty 10-s dome flats with $2\times2$
binning. They were dark-corrected during acquisition. I subtracted
the stored 100-DN pedestal once in 32-bit arithmetic, then measured
ten disjoint pairs at each exposure in a central $100\times100$
region starting at $(714,714)$, without alignment or normalization.

If photon noise dominates, the gain estimate is
\begin{equation}
g_{\rm ph}=\frac{\overline F_1+\overline F_2}{\sigma_{F_1-F_2}^2}.
\end{equation}
For the first 10-s pair, means of 27292.439 and 27332.640 DN and
a difference SD of 206.305 DN give $1.283~\mathrm{e^-\,DN^{-1}}$.
Table~\ref{tab:b_gain_pairs} lists all pairs. The 10-s mean is
$1.275\pm0.006~\mathrm{e^-\,DN^{-1}}$ (SEM; SD 0.019); the
0.2-s mean is $0.829\pm0.005~\mathrm{e^-\,DN^{-1}}$ (SD 0.014). The lower value from the faint flats is expected when additive noise
contributes a substantial fraction of the measured variance. This effect
biases the photon-only estimate downward without requiring a change in
the physical gain.

To account for this additive contribution, I fitted all twenty pairs
with a linear variance model:
\begin{align}
S&=(\overline F_1+\overline F_2)/2,\\
V&=\sigma_{F_1-F_2}^2/2=aS+c,\qquad g=1/a.
\end{align}
The unweighted fit gives $a=0.7751$, $c=251~\mathrm{DN^2}$, and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{g_{2\times2}=1.290\pm0.006~\mathrm{e^-\,DN^{-1}}}.
\end{equation}
The error is $\sigma_a/a^2$; it excludes calibration and region-selection
systematics. The intercept includes read and calibration noise.
Intermediate signal levels would provide a stronger linearity test.

\subsection{Saturation Limits}
Saturation can occur in the pixel well, analog electronics, or ADC.
The 16-bit digital ceiling is 65535 DN. At a dome-flat mean of
64376 DN, spatial SD falls to 63 DN (Table~\ref{tab:saturation}),
consistent with clipping or nonlinearity. Its maximum of 64457 DN
is close to the ceiling, but the original offset handling is unknown.
The STX-9000 specifications list a KAF-09000 sensor with nominal
$110000~\mathrm{e^-}$ full well and a 16-bit ADC \cite{camera}.
At the measured binned gain, $65535g\simeq8.45\times10^4~\mathrm{e^-}$.
The ADC is therefore the expected limit before the nominal pixel
well fills, provided the analog chain stays linear. An earlier analog limit remains possible because I did not measure the electronics or full-well capacity independently.

\section{Exercise C: Stellar Images and Photometry}
\subsection{Observations and Focus}
We observed Omicron Aquilae for focus and Ogma (HD 149026) for
repeatability and exposure-time tests through Johnson R. Wind
introduced pointing disturbances of up to about 5 arcseconds.
AstroImageJ calibration subtracted a master dark containing bias,
then divided by a normalized R-band dome flat:
\begin{equation}
I_{\rm cal}=(I_{\rm raw}-D_{\rm master})/F_{\rm norm}.
\end{equation}
I did not subtract a separate bias. Focus and repeatability use
$2\times2$ binning; the exposure-time series is unbinned.

The focus sequence used 6-s exposures, stepping from 17000 toward
$15000~\mu\mathrm{m}$. Table~\ref{tab:focus_measurements} lists the
AstroImageJ FWHMs; Image 5 is absent from the saved sequence.
FWHM decreases and then increases, locating best focus near
$\boxed{f_{\rm best}\approx16000~\mu\mathrm{m}}$. Repeated-position means and sample SDs are
$10.22\pm0.66$ pixels at 16000 and $11.72\pm0.63$ pixels at 17000.
The scatter at repeated positions indicates that seeing, tracking,
and profile measurement also affect the measured width, limiting the
precision with which the focus minimum can be determined.

The raw focus frames have peaks of 49146--49575 DN and 4--94 pixels
above 48000 DN in peak-centered $41\times41$ boxes. The flattened profiles are consistent with a nonlinear response
(Figure~\ref{fig:focus}). The sequence therefore identifies a best-focus
region, but the roughly 10-pixel minimum should not be interpreted as
the width of an unsaturated stellar image.

\subsection{Photometric Repeatability}
I measured fifteen individual 8-s Ogma frames with a 17-pixel
aperture and 34--67-pixel sky annulus. For net counts $C_i$,
\begin{align}
R_i&=C_i/t_i,\\
D_i&=100(R_i-\bar R)/\bar R,\\
s_R&=\sqrt{\sum_i(R_i-\bar R)^2/(N-1)}.
\end{align}
Figure~\ref{fig:repeatability} shows the count-rate deviations. The mean rate is $199592~\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$ and the sample SD is
$13691~\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$, giving $\boxed{100s_R/\bar R=6.86\%}$ scatter. The mean formal
AstroImageJ error, 0.107\%, is about 64 times smaller. However,
Ogma's flattened 48919--49598-DN cores (Figure~\ref{fig:repeatability_core}) make this an unsuitable test
of unsaturated precision. The 6.86\% is the scatter among individual frames, not the error on the mean.

To obtain a repeatability measurement that avoids the nonlinear Ogma
cores, I measured an isolated, unsaturated field star in the same
15 calibrated frames, without stacking or alignment. Python iterated
a positive-weight centroid in a $19\times19$ window, using the same
aperture and annulus with median sky subtraction. Its first array position was
$(221.07,1237.25)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:c_field_star}) and its peaks were 6332--9826 DN. Its mean rate
was $10887~\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$, with sample SD 145 and relative
scatter $\boxed{100s_R/\bar R=1.33\%}$ (deviations $-2.5$ to $+3.2$\%).

For an approximate statistical comparison I used
\begin{equation}
\sigma_C^2\simeq C/g+N_a s_b^2+\pi N_a^2s_b^2/(2N_b),
\end{equation}
with $g=1.290~\mathrm{e^-\,DN^{-1}}$, aperture and annulus pixel
counts $N_a,N_b$, and
$s_b=1.4826\,\mathrm{median}|B-\mathrm{median}(B)|$.
The final term approximates median-sky uncertainty for Gaussian noise.
The mean modeled fractional error is $\boxed{0.89\%}$; source photon noise alone gives
$\boxed{0.30\%}$. Both estimates are below the observed 1.33\% scatter, indicating that
source photon statistics alone cannot account for the variation.
Tracking, changes in transparency, flat-field residuals, and correlations
introduced during calibration may also contribute.

\subsection{Exposure Time, Width, and Count Rate}
I measured twelve unbinned images from 6 to 300 s using a
30-pixel aperture and 45--75-pixel sky annulus
(Table~\ref{tab:exposure_measurements}). Image 25 is 150 s according
to its header. Figure~\ref{fig:exposure_fwhm} shows that FWHM increases from 6.79 to 22.97 pixels, while
count rate falls from about $2.98\times10^5$ to
$6.99\times10^4~\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$. Stable unsaturated stellar
images should have approximately constant width and count rate.
The longer images are elongated (Figure~\ref{fig:ogma_long}) and have flattened cores,
consistent with tracking motion and nonlinear response.

Raw stellar-region peaks rise from 27401 DN at 6 s to 42552 DN at
8 s, then remain near 45900--46400 DN through 150 s (47300 DN at
300 s). No raw frame reaches 65535 DN. The roughly 45000-DN
calibrated plateau therefore does not demonstrate ADC clipping;
analog nonlinearity or another acquisition limit remains possible.
Different calibration and acquisition settings change the reported peak values, so the focus, repeatability, and dome images do not establish one common saturation threshold.

For Images 21--26, a 30-pixel aperture recovers 3.6--9.7\% more flux
than a 17-pixel aperture, although their different annuli also affect
the comparison. Although the larger aperture recovers additional flux, it does not
remove the decline in count rate. This indicates that aperture losses
alone cannot explain the observed behavior; nonlinear response,
tracking, and possible changes in transparency must also be considered. Individual
errors for this exposure-time series were unavailable.

\section{Exercise D: Color Photometry}
\subsection{M29 Measurements}
We observed M29 through B, V, and R with fifteen 30-s frames per
filter (450 s per band). Separate normalized dome flats were applied
in AstroImageJ. The saved R-band setup applies a master bias and
R flat with dark subtraction disabled; the master-flat combination
method was not recorded. The calibrated images were aligned and
averaged within each band.

I measured twelve stars with an 8-pixel aperture and 15--22-pixel
sky annulus. Stars 10 and 11 reach about 48981 DN in R and were
excluded, leaving ten. The finding-chart labels (Figure~\ref{fig:m29_finding_chart}) match Tables~\ref{tab:m29_counts} and \ref{tab:m29_colors}: Star 1 is aperture T1, and the other stars retain their AstroImageJ C-numbers.

For background-subtracted counts $C$ in an average stack,
$m_{\rm inst}=-2.5\log_{10}(C/t)$. Averaging retains the single-frame
signal scale, so $t=30$ s; the equal exposure factor cancels:
\begin{align}
(B-V)_{\rm inst}&=-2.5\log_{10}(C_B/C_V),\\
(V-R)_{\rm inst}&=-2.5\log_{10}(C_V/C_R).
\end{align}
I propagated the exported formal count errors using
\begin{equation}
\sigma_m=\frac{2.5}{\ln10}\frac{\sigma_C}{C},
\end{equation}
then added independent band variances for each color. The shared V
measurement gives $\mathrm{Cov}(B-V,V-R)=-\sigma_V^2$.
These formal errors exclude calibration and atmospheric systematics. Averaging fifteen independent images reduces random noise by $\sqrt{15}$, but shared calibration errors and alignment correlations remain. Because the saved stacks lack a verified variance model, the exported errors are retained as formal estimates and are not used to infer intrinsic color scatter.

\subsection{NGC 7129 Composite}
The Python pipeline retained 18 B, 15 V, and 20 R dome flats,
excluding B frames 9 and 19 and V frames 4, 6, 8, 16, and 20.
Each flat was median-normalized; the median-combined master was
renormalized to unity. Science frames were already dark-corrected.
I removed their stored 100-DN pedestal by adding the signed
\texttt{PEDESTAL=-100} value, then divided by the matching flat,
without further bias or dark subtraction.

I aligned to the first V frame with \texttt{astroalign} and
median-combined fifteen frames per filter. To reduce a broad
background gradient, I subtracted a model made with a
$31\times31$ median filter and Gaussian smoothing
($\sigma=120$ pixels). Each channel was median-subtracted, clipped
at zero, scaled by its 99.5th percentile, and stretched with an
inverse hyperbolic sine. R, V, and B map to red, green, and blue.

Figure~\ref{fig:ngc7129_final} shows the nebula's structure and bright central region.
Because the channels were scaled independently, the displayed colors
are qualitative. In addition, the smooth background model may remove
some faint extended nebulosity. These processing choices limit the
image to a morphological comparison rather than a quantitative color or
surface-brightness measurement. Masking both stars and nebulosity when
estimating the background would better preserve the diffuse emission.

\subsection{Separations and Plate Scale}
AstroImageJ measured HR6200, HR7751, and HR8815 in three exposures
each. HR6200 and HR8815 used 9-pixel primary and 4-pixel companion
apertures with 40--60-pixel annuli; HR7751 used 6-pixel apertures
and 19--26-pixel annuli. For HR7310, Python used a 12-pixel-radius
background-subtracted centroid for the primary and a robust Gaussian
plus planar-background fit to the faint companion in a $17\times17$
box.

For each exposure,
\begin{equation}
d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2},\quad p=\theta/d,
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the matching WDS separation \cite{wds}.
Table~\ref{tab:e_verified} gives the repeated measurements.
Figure~\ref{fig:e_separation_plot} compares pixel and catalog separations. The four pair means range from 1.232 to
$1.283~\mathrm{arcsec\,pixel^{-1}}$, exceeding the repeat SEMs.
Equal weighting gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\bar p=1.251\pm0.012~\mathrm{arcsec\,pixel^{-1}}},
\end{equation}
with between-target SD 0.023 and SEM $\mathrm{SD}/\sqrt4$.
The reference measurements span catalog epochs from 2015 to 2021 and
have no quoted separation uncertainties in the adopted tables. Changes
in binary separation, centroid bias, and field distortion may therefore
contribute to the spread. With only four verified pairs, these
contributions cannot be separated.
HR7751's longer pixel baseline gives the most repeatable result
and agrees with the mean.

\subsection{Image Width and Field of View}
Six unsaturated companion measurements in HR6200 and HR8815
(peaks 1273--1702 DN) have mean FWHM 3.471 pixels and sample
SD 0.318. Multiplying by the plate scale gives an observed width
of $\boxed{w=4.34~\mathrm{arcsec}}$, with sample scatter $\boxed{s_w=0.40~\mathrm{arcsec}}$; the scale
SEM contributes 0.04 arcsecond to the mean width. Because focus, tracking, and pixel sampling also broaden stellar
images, this value describes the observed angular image width rather
than an isolated measurement of atmospheric seeing.

The side length of the nominal full-array field is
\begin{equation}
L=1528\bar p/60
\end{equation}
where $L$ is in arcminutes. The field spans $\boxed{31.87\times31.87~\mathrm{arcmin}}$; each side has a
0.29-arcminute contribution from the scale SEM. The side errors
are fully correlated. This excludes distortion and cropped borders.

\section*{Conclusion}
I have characterized the CCD response and examined how calibration and
observing conditions affect stellar measurements. The paired bias
frames give a read noise of $10.045\pm0.007$ DN, while the joint
flat-pair fit gives a binned gain of
$1.290\pm0.006~\mathrm{e^-\,DN^{-1}}$. Dark signal increases with
exposure time and temperature, consistent with a thermal contribution.
However, the unmatched bias and uncertain detector temperatures limit
the interpretation of the inferred dark current and activation energy.

The stellar observations locate best focus near $16000~\mu\mathrm{m}$,
although nonlinear cores limit the measured minimum width. An
unsaturated field star shows 1.33\% repeatability scatter, exceeding
the source photon-noise prediction. The broadening and declining count
rate in longer Ogma exposures further demonstrate the combined effects
of nonlinear response, tracking, and aperture losses. The M29
instrumental colors identify Star 8 as relatively red, while the NGC
7129 composite reveals nebular structure without providing calibrated
colors. Finally, four verified binaries give a plate scale of
$1.251\pm0.012$ arcseconds per pixel and a
$31.87\times31.87$-arcminute field. More complete temperature records,
unsaturated stellar exposures, and additional verified binary
measurements would provide stronger constraints on these results.
